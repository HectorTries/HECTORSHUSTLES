The first draft you'll actually enjoy writing

25 August 2026 • Productivity

Use case: Dictating an entire first draft out loud in one flow — then cleaning it up in minutes instead of hours.

I used to believe writing meant sitting down, staring at a cursor, and producing. You know the ritual: open the doc, watch the blinking line, feel the pressure, write a sentence, delete it, check Twitter, come back, write two more.

The problem was never that I couldn't write. It was that I was trying to do two jobs at once — thinking and performing for an invisible editor — and the blank page kept reminding me I wasn't good enough yet.

So I flipped it. Now the first draft isn't typed at all. It's spoken.

I pick a topic, hit record, and talk for ten minutes like I'm explaining it to a friend over coffee. No structure, no self-editing, no "is this sentence good?" — just the whole thing, start to finish, out loud. My hands are free, so there's nothing to fidget with. My voice finds the through-line the way typing never does, because talking is how I actually think.

Then Wispr Flow does the boring part: it turns that rambling monologue into clean, readable text while I'm still walking back from the kitchen.

And here's the thing that surprised me — the draft is good. Not because I'm a great speaker, but because a spoken first draft has natural rhythm. It has personality. It sounds like a person, because it was one. When I typed, I edited myself into blandness before the second paragraph. Talking skips all of that and hands me something with a voice already in it.

The editing pass is where the magic happens anyway. I read it back, cut the waffle, reorder two paragraphs, sharpen the ending — twenty minutes of real editing instead of two hours of fake writing. The draft becomes the cheapest part of the process instead of the scariest.

It also made me finally stop avoiding the big pieces. A 2,000-word essay used to feel like a mountain. Now it's just... a few longer coffee chats, stitched together. Same words, same me, but none of the dread.

If the blank page has been winning lately, try not writing at all. Talk it out first — let the words land on the page for you, then turn the editor back on.
