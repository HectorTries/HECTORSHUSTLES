Write Build-in-Public Updates Without Stopping the Build

7 September 2026 • Productivity

I keep a public log of what I'm building. It's good for accountability, it brings in readers, and every so often it turns into a customer. But there's one problem: stopping to write the update breaks the flow. You sit down to record what you did, and suddenly you're re-reading code instead of writing about it.

That's the exact gap Wispr Flow fills for me. I don't write updates anymore. I talk them, while I keep working.

Here's what my loop looks like now. I'm testing a video tool on a server, waiting on a job to finish, staring at logs. That's dead time. So I press the button, and I say: "The new pipeline finally handles a full wedding day. It labels every clip in one pass instead of folder by folder. Next problem is disk space, the raw footage is bigger than the server." Wispr Flow turns that ramble into clean text on the page, formatted and ready.

The magic is that the update gets written in the exact moment it's true. Not at 10pm when I've forgotten what the win was, or why it mattered. The honest details are still in my head because I'm literally living them. The draft comes out specific, which is the whole difference between a build-in-public post people read and a diary entry nobody clicks.

I also use it for the ugly parts, which matter more. Failures are the best content in build-in-public, and they're the easiest to lose. "Upload two gigs and the server died. Out of memory. Going to stream the upload in chunks instead." Thirty seconds of talking and I have a draft of the most honest paragraph I'll write all week.

Another habit that stuck: dictating the closer while the work is still fresh. The last line of my posts is usually a recap of what actually got done. If I dictate it at 6pm instead of reconstructing it at midnight, it takes ninety seconds and it's accurate.

The result? More updates, better updates, and none of them cost me a working hour. The writing happens in the gaps between the work, not instead of it.

If you run a project journal, a dev log, or any kind of public build, stop typing your updates and start talking them. You'll write more often, and you'll write the truth.
