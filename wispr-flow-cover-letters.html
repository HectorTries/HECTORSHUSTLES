I Wrote a Cover Letter in 10 Minutes — Without Staring at a Blank Page

7 October 2026 • Productivity

The job posting had been open in a tab for four days. Marketing executive, hybrid, genuinely interesting. Everything was ready — CV updated, portfolio linked — except the cover letter. Every blank document produced exactly one sentence ("I am writing to apply for…") and then static.

Last time I applied for jobs I lost an entire Sunday to three cover letters. Rewriting the same paragraph six times and ending up with something that sounded like a robot wearing a suit. This time I tried something different. I closed the laptop, opened Wispr Flow on my phone, and just talked about why I wanted the job.

The 10-minute talking cover letter

Here's roughly what I said: "Okay, so why do I actually want this job. The company does sustainability marketing, which is what I did in my last role for two years — I ran the email campaigns and grew the list from four thousand to twelve thousand. I also handled the social content and one campaign got picked up by two industry blogs. I'm good at the analytics side too, I lived in spreadsheets, weekly reporting, A/B testing subject lines. The hybrid setup suits me and the salary band is a step up, which matters because rent. Oh, and I should mention I managed a small freelancer budget — shows I can handle money."

Rambling and unstructured — but every fact a cover letter needs was in there, with real numbers I'd have forgotten while fussing over formatting.

Wispr Flow turned it into clean text with the numbers intact. Five minutes of reshaping into three paragraphs, and it was done.

Why voice wins for cover letters

Cover letters fail when they sound written instead of said. Hiring managers skim dozens of "I am a highly motivated self-starter" letters — what stands out is a human voice with specific proof. But typing makes you perform — formal words, hedged claims, and the honest sentence deleted.

Talking skips the performance. Saying "I grew the list from four thousand to twelve thousand" out loud feels natural; typing it feels like bragging, so people water it down. The ramble also surfaces forgotten wins — I'd blanked on the freelancer budget until I heard myself say it, and that line shows management potential.

The result

Ten minutes, one talking session, cover letter sent. No lost Sunday, no sixth rewrite, no robot in a suit. The letter sounded like me on a good day — specific, direct, with numbers doing the heavy lifting.

Got a job tab that's been open for days? Talk your way through it before typing a word. Say why you want it, say what you've done, let the ramble find the proof — then tidy and send.
