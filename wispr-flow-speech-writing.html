I Wrote My Best Man Speech by Talking to Myself in the Car

5 October 2026 • Productivity

My best mate's wedding is three weeks away. I'm his best man. And until last Tuesday, my speech was a blank Google Doc titled "speech FINAL v1" that had been blank for a month.

Every time I sat down to write it, I froze. Typing felt too formal — everything came out stiff, like a school essay. The funny stories sounded flat. The sincere bit sounded cheesy. I deleted more than I wrote, and the wedding kept getting closer.

So I tried something different. I opened Wispr Flow on my phone, parked in the driveway after work, and just talked.

The car-park first draft

Here's roughly what I said: "Okay. Open with the holiday story — the one where he locked us out of the Airbnb in Spain and we slept on the beach. Then how we've known each other since we were eleven, football trials, he was rubbish but the coach liked him. Then the serious bit — he's the bloke who drove forty minutes at midnight when my car broke down on the M1. Then toast. Keep it under five minutes."

Ten minutes of rambling. False starts, half-sentences, me laughing at my own story halfway through. I didn't edit a word — just talked the way I'd actually talk standing up with a microphone.

Wispr Flow turned it into a clean draft. The stories were in order. The rambling bits were trimmed into actual sentences. The structure — funny opening, friendship middle, sincere close, toast — was already there, because that's just how I naturally told it.

Why voice works for speeches

A speech is spoken, not written. When you type one, you're translating twice: thought → written word → spoken word. Every translation loses something. Jokes that kill out loud die on the page because you over-edit them. Sincere lines sound stiff because you're writing, not feeling.

Dictating skips a step. You hear your own rhythm as you go. The Spain story landed because I was laughing while telling it — I could feel where the pauses go, where the room would laugh. You can't get that from a keyboard.

It also kills the blank-page problem. Nobody freezes when talking to themselves in a car. The words come out messy, but messy words on a transcript beat perfect words that never get written.

The result

One car-park session gave me 80% of the speech. I read it back the next day, tweaked a few lines, timed it at four and a half minutes. Done. The month of staring at a blank doc produced nothing; ten minutes of talking produced a speech I'm actually looking forward to giving.

If you've got a wedding, birthday, leaving do, or any moment where you need to stand up and say something real — don't write it. Say it first.
