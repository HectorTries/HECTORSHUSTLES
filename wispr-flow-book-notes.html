I Actually Remember What I Read Now

22 September 2026 • Productivity

I read a lot of books. For a while, I remembered almost none of them.

I'd finish something great, put it down feeling inspired, and two weeks later someone would ask what it was about — and I'd blank. "Uh, it was really good? You should read it?" Not exactly a glowing review from someone who just spent six hours on it.

Highlighting didn't help. My Kindle is full of highlights I've never looked at again. Note-taking apps were worse. Stopping mid-chapter to type a clean summary killed the whole rhythm, so I just stopped doing it.

Now I talk to the book instead.

When a passage hits me, I tap Wispr Flow and say what I think in ten seconds flat.

"That bit about attention being the real budget — not time. Rich people buy back attention, poor systems steal it. Reminds me of my phone habit in the mornings."

"Chapter three idea: constraints force creativity, like the author writing in 45-minute train rides. I should try that for my newsletter."

No formatting, no complete sentences, no switching apps. Just my raw reaction while it's fresh. Wispr Flow turns the ramble into clean text I can actually use later.

Here's why voice works for this: reading is already slow and quiet. Typing notes makes it slower and more formal. Talking keeps you in the flow — you react the way you'd tell a friend about it, which is exactly how memory works. You remember what you explain, not what you highlight.

The payoff shows up weeks later. Before a call or a writing session, I skim my dictated notes and the ideas come straight back — with my own take attached, not just a quote. Last month I pulled three newsletter angles and one client story straight from books I'd "forgotten" I'd read.

I finished 11 books this year and can actually talk about all of them. That's never happened before.

If your highlights go to die in the cloud, try this: next time something clicks while you read, don't highlight it. Say it.
